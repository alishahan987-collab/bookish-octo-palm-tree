# Why Do We Hate the Sound of Our Own Recorded Voice?

**Length:** 5:06  ·  **Format:** 1080×1920, 60 fps, H.264 + AAC

## Hook options

1. There is a version of your voice that everyone you know has heard... except you.
2. The moment you hear your own voice on a recording, your brain says: that's not me. Your brain is wrong.
3. Why does your own voice make you cringe, when everyone else's sounds completely normal?

**Chosen:** 1 — it opens a curiosity gap in the first 3 seconds and makes it personal to every viewer.

## Script

- **Narrator:** There is a version of your voice that everyone you know has heard.
- **Narrator:** Your friends. Your family. Your teachers.
- **Narrator:** Everyone... except you.
- **Narrator:** So why do so many of us hate the sound of our own recorded voice?
- **Narrator:** It usually starts with something simple. Like sending a voice message.
- **Stickman (inner voice):** Hey! It's me. Call me back when you get this!
- **Narrator:** Then, just out of curiosity, you press play.
- **Stickman (as recorded):** Hey! It's me. Call me back when you get this!
- **Stickman (inner voice):** Wait... is that what I sound like?
- **Narrator:** If that made you cringe, you're not alone. Lots of people can't stand hearing their own recorded voice.
- **Narrator:** But here's the strange part. The recording isn't lying to you.
- **Narrator:** It's actually much closer to how everyone else hears you.
- **Narrator:** To understand why, let's look at what happens every time you speak.
- **Narrator:** When you talk, your vocal cords vibrate.
- **Narrator:** Those vibrations push sound waves out of your mouth, through the air, and into your ears.
- **Narrator:** That's the first road. And it's exactly how other people hear you.
- **Narrator:** But there's a second road. One that only you have.
- **Narrator:** The same vibrations travel through your throat, your jaw, and the bones of your skull, straight to your inner ear.
- **Narrator:** Scientists call this bone conduction.
- **Narrator:** And your skull is especially good at carrying the low, deep notes.
- **Narrator:** So the voice you hear in your head is a mix of both roads. Deeper. Fuller. Richer.
- **Narrator:** It's the voice you've heard every single day of your life.
- **Narrator:** A microphone, though, only catches the first road.
- **Narrator:** It can't hear the vibrations inside your skull.
- **Narrator:** So when you play it back, those deep notes are gone.
- **Narrator:** What's left sounds thinner, higher, and strangely unfamiliar.
- **Narrator:** Like a stranger doing a bad impression of you.
- **Narrator:** Here's a rough idea of the difference. First, something like the voice in your head.
- **Stickman (inner voice):** This is how I sound to me.
- **Narrator:** And now, the recording.
- **Stickman (as recorded):** This is how I sound to everyone else.
- **Narrator:** You can test this right now. Put your fingers in your ears and say something.
- **Narrator:** Go on. Nobody's watching.
- **Narrator:** Hear how deep and boomy it sounds? Blocking your ears cuts out most of the air road, so you mostly hear the bone road.
- **Narrator:** That's the hidden bass you never knew was there.
- **Narrator:** But bone conduction only explains why a recording sounds different. It doesn't fully explain why it makes us cringe.
- **Narrator:** In 1966, two psychologists, Philip Holzman and Clyde Rousey, studied this exact feeling.
- **Narrator:** They called it voice confrontation.
- **Narrator:** When people heard someone else's recording, they paid attention to what was being said.
- **Narrator:** But when they heard their own voice, they focused on how it sounded. And they didn't like it.
- **Narrator:** The researchers suggested that recordings reveal things we don't notice while we're talking. A nervous tone. A hesitation. Little clues about how we come across.
- **Narrator:** Things that don't match the confident voice we hear in our heads.
- **Narrator:** Something similar happens with photos.
- **Narrator:** In a 1977 experiment, researchers showed a group of students two photos of their own face: a normal one, and a mirror-flipped one.
- **Narrator:** Most preferred the flipped one: the face they see in the mirror every day. But their close friends preferred the normal photo.
- **Narrator:** Each side liked the version they were used to.
- **Narrator:** Psychologists call this the mere exposure effect. The more familiar something is, the more we tend to like it.
- **Narrator:** And your voice works the same way. You're used to the version in your head, so the real one sounds wrong.
- **Narrator:** But here's the twist.
- **Narrator:** In 2013, researchers asked eighty people to rate how attractive a set of recorded voices sounded. Their own voice was secretly mixed in.
- **Narrator:** The result? People rated their own voice as more attractive than other listeners did.
- **Narrator:** And more attractive than everyone else's voice.
- **Narrator:** So maybe the problem isn't your voice at all. Maybe it's knowing that it's yours.
- **Narrator:** The good news? The cringe fades. Radio hosts, singers, YouTubers... people who hear their own voice every day often get used to it.
- **Narrator:** The more you hear it, the more normal it sounds.
- **Narrator:** So next time you hear a recording of yourself, remember: that voice isn't worse. It's just the one you've never heard from the outside.
- **Narrator:** It's the voice your friends recognize instantly.
- **Stickman:** Hey!
- **Narrator:** That's the voice that makes someone turn around when you call their name.
- **Narrator:** Everyone in your life already knows that voice.
- **Narrator:** Maybe it's time you got to know it too.
- **Narrator:** So... do you hate your recorded voice? Tell me in the comments.
- **Narrator:** And follow for more strange questions about your brain.
